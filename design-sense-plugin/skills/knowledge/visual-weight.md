# Visual weight

Judge prominence relative to its surroundings. A broad tinted region can dominate a small bold title; a repeated badge can become louder than either. Before strengthening the important element, find what is already competing with it.

A palette supplies values, not priorities. Assign emphasis to roles before distributing a hue: body text, secondary content, surfaces, actions, selection and status. For a utilitarian task without a contrary visual commitment, neutral surfaces and ordinary text leave room for meaningful emphasis. An accent belongs where it identifies an action, state or intended expression; possessing a palette does not give every region a reason to use it. Tinting each role with the same accent can make unrelated things seem related and weaken the important signal. A restrained single-hue system can still work when luminance, area, placement and non-color cues distinguish those roles. More hues are not a substitute for that distinction.

Selection needs to be recognizable. It seldom needs fill, stripe, colored text and badge to repeat the same message. Keep enough cues to locate the selection, including a cue beyond hue where needed, and let the content regain attention. Neutral selection is useful in some navigation; the brief or subject may justify stronger color elsewhere.

For peers, try weight, spacing and placement before inventing another text size. A genuinely different reading role may deserve a larger size. Supporting text must remain legible; tiny, faint metadata is not a successful hierarchy.

Reduce the screen's detail and see which masses remain. Judge the total colored area with selection, primary action and surrounding surfaces visible together. If a sidebar wins over the current task, lower its competing weight rather than escalating every content control. Change one variable, then inspect at working size with focus and selected states visible. Urgent conditions may deliberately break the usual hierarchy.
